Placebo surgery — sometimes called sham surgery — is a research design in which participants undergo a simulated operative procedure that mimics every sensory and psychological aspect of a real operation except the presumed therapeutic maneuver. The goal is to separate the specific biological effect of the surgery from the nonspecific effects of anesthesia, incision, hospitalization, and the patient’s expectation of benefit.

The most famous example comes from arthroscopic knee surgery for osteoarthritis. In a 2002 multicenter trial published in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, patients randomized to lavage, débridement, or a sham procedure (skin incisions only) reported identical improvements in pain and function at two years. Subsequent sham‑controlled trials of vertebroplasty for osteoporotic fractures, percutaneous coronary intervention for stable angina, and even deep brain stimulation for Parkinson’s disease have produced similarly sobering results.

Mechanistically, placebo surgery harnesses the same neurobiological pathways as other placebos: expectation‑driven release of endogenous opioids, dopamine, and cannabinoids; conditioning from prior medical encounters; and the ritualistic theater of the operating room. Functional imaging studies show that sham incisions can activate pain‑modulatory circuits in the anterior cingulate and periaqueductal gray, producing measurable analgesia.

Ethically, placebo surgery sits at a tense intersection. The Declaration of Helsinki permits placebo controls only when no proven therapy exists, yet many sham‑surgery trials test procedures already in widespread use. Proponents argue that without a surgical placebo, ineffective operations continue to expose patients to real risks — infection, bleeding, anesthesia complications — while consuming resources. Critics contend that deceiving patients about an invasive procedure violates informed consent and erodes trust.

Regulators and journals now demand rigorous justification for sham arms, often requiring that the sham be minimally invasive, that participants be fully debriefed, and that the scientific question be unanswerable by less risky designs. As evidence accumulates, several once‑standard operations have been downgraded or abandoned, illustrating how placebo surgery can serve as a powerful corrective force in medicine — provided its use remains transparent, scientifically necessary, and ethically scrutinized.
