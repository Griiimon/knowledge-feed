Beneath the frozen surface of Antarctica's coastal waters, a little-known phenomenon occurs: brinicles — underwater icicles that form in one of the planet's most extreme environments. Unlike their atmospheric counterparts, brinicles develop entirely beneath the sea ice, growing downward from the underside of ice shelves in slow motion.

The process begins when seawater freezes. Salt is expelled from the forming ice crystals, creating concentrated brine that is denser and colder than the surrounding seawater. This supercooled brine, sometimes referred to as 'airfrost' when it contacts air, sinks due to its high density. As it descends, it triggers rapid freezing in the seawater it encounters, forming a sheath of ice around the descending brine channel. Over weeks or months, this sheath elongates into a tube-like structure — a brinicle.

The rate of growth depends on temperature, salinity, and water movement. In stable conditions, brinicles can reach several meters in length. Some have been observed engulfing objects on the seafloor, encasing them in thick layers of ice like natural glass paperweights.

For decades, brinicles were considered too ephemeral or hidden to study effectively. They gained broader scientific attention after the early 2000s, when researchers began using time-lapse cameras and ROVs to capture their formation. One notable case involved a brinicle forming near Hidden Lake, Antarctica, documented by marine biologist Dr. Douglas Gardner and later featured in BBC’s *Frozen Planet* series.

Despite advances, much remains unknown. The impact of climate change on brinicle frequency or distribution is unclear. Some scientists hypothesize that reduced sea ice extent might alter brine rejection patterns, potentially reducing brinicle occurrences. Others suggest that increased ice formation in certain areas could enhance them temporarily.

Because brinicles occur in remote, frigid waters, they are difficult to monitor consistently. No long-term datasets exist, and observations remain opportunistic. However, their existence challenges assumptions about how water and ice interact in polar regions, offering new insights into ocean circulation and sea ice dynamics.

While visually striking, brinicles serve a functional role: by exporting dense, cold water to deeper ocean layers, they contribute to vertical mixing and global thermohaline circulation. Thus, these ephemeral ice forms may play a subtle but significant part in regulating Earth’s climate system.
