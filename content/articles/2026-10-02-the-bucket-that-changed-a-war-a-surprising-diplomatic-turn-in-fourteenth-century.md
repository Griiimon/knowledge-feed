The so‑called War of the Bucket is usually introduced as a clash begun by a trivial quarrel: in the autumn of 1325 a Modenese soldier seized a bucket from a well in Bologna, an act that provoked a Bolognese response in which the soldier was killed. Modena responded with a full‑scale invasion, and the two city‑states fought a series of battles over the next eight years. The war is notable for its origins, but its consequences are less often highlighted.

What followed was an early example of formal diplomatic mediation on a large scale. Pope John XXII intervened in 1332, sending a legate to arrange a truce that would be formalized at Ferrara in 1333. The negotiations produced a treaty that not only returned the contested bucket but also established clear terms for the cessation of hostilities, the exchange of hostages, and the restoration of damaged territories. The peace of Ferrara introduced a pattern of written agreements, neutral venues, and papal oversight that later Italian states emulated during the series of balance‑of‑power treaties of the 15th century.

The diplomatic character of the conflict is evident in the treaty’s language, which repeatedly refers to mutual obligations rather than feudal claims. While the war itself caused considerable damage to the surrounding countryside, the diplomatic framework created by the peace helped reduce the likelihood of immediate retaliation, setting a precedent for resolving disputes through negotiation rather than prolonged fighting. This approach was later refined by the Italian condottieri system, where mercenary captains often negotiated contracts that included clauses for payment, conduct, and dispute resolution.

It is uncertain whether the original bucket still exists; Bolognese records state that the vessel was placed in the city’s Palazzo Comunale as a trophy, but later renovations may have moved or replaced it. The bucket’s symbolic value, however, persisted as a reminder of the peace’s emphasis on concrete restitution over abstract honor.

In sum, the War of the Bucket is remarkable not for its cause but for the diplomatic mechanisms it inaugurated. The peace of Ferrara showed that even deeply rooted municipal rivalries could be settled through structured mediation, an innovation that resonated throughout the subsequent history of Italian statecraft.
