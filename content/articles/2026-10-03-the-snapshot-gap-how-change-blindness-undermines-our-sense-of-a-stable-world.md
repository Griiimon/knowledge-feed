People often assume that seeing is continuous: the world flows past in a smooth stream, and we register every change. Change blindness research demonstrates that this assumption is wrong. When visual information is disrupted—by a blink, a saccade, or a brief blank—the brain does not maintain a detailed record of the scene. Instead, it constructs a reduced‑resolution representation that can miss large alterations.

In experiments, participants watch a video of two actors exchanging objects. A single flash occurs between the two scenes, and many fail to notice that the object has changed hands or been replaced. The failure is not due to inattention alone; the visual system simply does not capture the transformation. The same effect appears with more consequential changes, such as a person disappearing from a group photograph or a tool being swapped in a tool‑use demonstration.

The implications extend beyond laboratory tasks. Drivers may overlook a pedestrian stepping onto a crosswalk because their attention is focused elsewhere, even though the change is large enough to be visible. In legal contexts, eyewitnesses often report confidence in details that were never encoded, because memory is later filled in by plausible inferences. Safety protocols in aviation and medicine also rely on the assumption that operators notice critical changes; change blindness suggests that additional monitoring mechanisms are necessary.

Why does the brain adopt such a sparse representation? One hypothesis is that maintaining a full‑detail record would be computationally expensive. The visual system therefore prioritizes salient features and expects that changes will be noticed at the next fixation or saccade. This strategy works well when changes are gradual, but it leaves large gaps when disruption occurs.

It remains uncertain how much change blindness is mitigated by training, expertise, or the presence of predictive cues. Nonetheless, the phenomenon illustrates that perception is not a video feed but a series of snapshots, with blind spots that can have real consequences. Recognizing these gaps encourages the design of environments and procedures that compensate for the brain’s inherent limitations.
