Alfred Wegener proposed that continents had drifted, but he could not identify a credible mechanism. For decades, the hypothesis remained speculative because no process explained how large lithospheric sections could move. The key evidence emerged when magnetic anomalies on the ocean floor displayed symmetric patterns of reversed polarity, indicating that new crust is created at spreading ridges and pushes plates outward. This seafloor spreading concept showed that the lithosphere is broken into discrete plates. Later, seismic tomography revealed that dense oceanic slabs subduct into the mantle, generating a downward pull that drags the overlying plate toward the trench, a force now identified as slab pull. Quantitative models combined measurements of plate velocities with mantle flow calculations, confirming that slab pull accounts for the majority of the motion, while ridge push contributes at divergent boundaries. Although mantle convection still influences localized deformation, the dominant driver is the weight of sinking slabs. This shift in understanding unified observations of earthquake zones, volcanic arcs, and mountain ranges, which align with plate boundaries rather than uniform upwelling. It also clarified that the lithosphere behaves as a rigid outer shell floating on a more ductile asthenosphere, with convection cells playing a secondary, supportive role. The acceptance of slab pull as the primary force demonstrated that Earth’s surface evolution results from the interaction of plate boundaries and deep mantle processes, rather than a single global upwelling pattern.

The theory explains why earthquakes occur primarily along plate boundaries, where stress accumulates as plates slide past or collide. It also accounts for the distribution of volcanoes, which cluster near subduction zones where magma generated from melting slabs rises to the surface. Moreover, the motion of plates has reshaped continents over geological time, creating ocean basins and reorganizing landmasses.

The recognition of slab pull also spurred investigations into the rheology of the asthenosphere, leading to estimates of viscosity that are several orders of magnitude lower than earlier assumptions. It prompted the development of numerical simulations that reproduce plate motions, confirming that localized traction at subduction zones dominates global dynamics. While some researchers still argue for alternative mechanisms such as mantle plume buoyancy, the preponderance of evidence supports slab pull as the chief force.

Current satellite gravimetry and GPS measurements continue to refine the quantitative contribution of slab pull versus ridge push, but the fundamental conclusion remains unchanged.

These ongoing observations underscore that plate tectonics, while conceptually simple, involves complex interactions between surface and deep Earth processes that are still being quantified. The field continues to evolve with new data.
