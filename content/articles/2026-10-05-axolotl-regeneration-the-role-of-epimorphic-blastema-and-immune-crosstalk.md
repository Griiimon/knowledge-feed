Axolotls are famous for regrowing entire limbs, spinal cord segments, heart tissue, and portions of the brain. What sets them apart is not merely the extent of regrowth but the seamless integration of the regenerated tissue with existing structures. The process begins with the formation of a blastema—a mass of dedifferentiated progenitor cells that proliferate and redifferentiate into the missing cell types. In axolotls, blastema cells retain embryonic‑like plasticity, allowing them to recapitulate patterning events that normally occur during development.

What is less appreciated is the precise coordination between blastema expansion and the animal’s immune system. Recent studies have shown that axolotls recruit a population of macrophages to the injury site that secrete growth factors and modulate inflammatory signaling. This immune involvement appears to prevent fibrosis, the scar‑forming process that typically blocks regeneration in mammals. The exact molecular pathways linking macrophage activity to blastema proliferation remain uncertain, but candidate factors include BMP antagonists and fibroblast growth factor (FGF) family members.

Another surprising aspect of axolotl regeneration is the limited plasticity of their blastema. While they can rebuild complex structures, the blastema does not indiscriminately generate ectopic tissues. For example, a limb blastema will produce only limb‑specific cell types, not gill or neural tissue. This specificity suggests an intrinsic positional information system, possibly mediated by gradient cues such as Hox gene expression patterns that are re‑established during regeneration.

Understanding these mechanisms could inform strategies to coax human tissues toward regeneration. Unlike axolotls, mammals often form scar tissue after injury, largely due to rapid fibroblast activation and chronic inflammatory responses. By identifying the axolotl’s immune‑blastema cross‑talk, researchers may pinpoint therapeutic targets to shift mammalian healing toward a regenerative trajectory.

Key uncertainties persist: the precise timeline of macrophage recruitment, the contribution of specific cytokine signals, and how positional identity is restored in larger organisms. Resolving these questions will require comparative analyses across urodeles and an integrated view of epigenetics, cell signaling, and immunology.
